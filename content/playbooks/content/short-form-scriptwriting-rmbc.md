Do the legwork before writing a line; copy comes last by design.

1. Research the prospect until you can speak as one of their tribe: who they are, the pain, the outcome they want, what they've tried, what they liked and hated about it, the horror stories. Steal their exact words from reviews and forums (five-star = what they love about alternatives, one-star = what they hate).
2. Mechanism — pin the unique mechanism of the problem (the missing piece, why past solutions failed) AND the unique mechanism of the solution (why this works where others didn't). One without the other is a claim, not a reframe. For regulated categories the mechanism must be source, heritage or process — never efficacy.
3. Brief — answer the brief questions by writing pasteable copy chunks: audience, top pains, the one big promise, existing solutions and why they fail, the mechanism, the product, the discovery story. The brief becomes half the draft.
4. Copy — assemble in a fixed order: lead (hook plus big promise) → background or origin story → mechanism of the problem → mechanism of the solution → product build-up and reveal → close (benefits → life with it → value → call to action). Order is load-bearing; belief is built in sequence.

For short-form video, an overlay rides on that spine: open out of left field to buy three seconds of curiosity; frame the problem the unexpected way; demonstrate the solution rather than assert it; let entertainment carry it so it is watched to the end; every line drives the one central offer.

Quality bar: the prospect's own words are in the script; a two-part mechanism is explicit; it opens on something concrete, never the product; one offer; reads like a story a person would tell, not an ad. Grade the finished piece on hook and hold rate.

Failure modes: writing copy first; asserting instead of building a mechanism; rearranging the section order; a left-field hook with no follow-through; losing the one offer.
