Alternatives to hiring a marketing manager: the five real options

Tom Hall-Taylor · 7 August 2026 · 6 min read

If you don’t want to hire a full-time marketing manager, you have five real alternatives: a fractional CMO (senior strategy, a few hours a month), an agency (a team on retainer), freelancers (one specialist per channel), DIY software (you do the work, tools make it faster), or AI marketing agents (scheduled runs that do the daily routine and report back to you).

None of them is best for everyone. The right one depends on whether your problem is deciding what to do or actually doing it every day — and on how much of your own time you’re willing to spend managing whoever does it.

Here’s how each one actually works, and how to pick in about ten minutes.

First, be clear about the job you’re hiring for

Most founders say “I need a marketing manager.” What they usually mean is one of three different jobs:

  1. Decide what to do. Which customers, which message, which channels, in what order.
  2. Do it, every day. Write the emails. Watch the ads. Post the content. Chase the leads.
  3. Check whether it worked. Get the numbers right, and change course when they say to.

A full-time manager is one person expected to do all three. That’s why the hire is hard, and why it so often disappoints: the person who’s brilliant at deciding is rarely the person who wants to be in the ad account every morning.

Once you know which of the three jobs is actually missing, the choice below gets much easier.

Option 1: A fractional CMO

A senior marketer, a few hours a month, on your strategy.

Good at: job one — deciding. You get someone who has done it before, tells you what to stop doing, and sets the plan.

Weak at: job two. They will not be in your inbox at 8:30am doing the work. If you hire a fractional CMO and nobody executes, you end up with an excellent plan and the same results.

Choose it when: you have people who can do the work but nobody who knows what the work should be.

Option 2: An agency

A team on retainer, usually per channel — ads, or email, or SEO.

Good at: getting real execution moving quickly, with people who do this all day.

Weak at: two things. First, your account is one of many, so attention moves around. Second, most agencies are hired per channel, which means nobody owns the whole picture — your ads agency doesn’t know what your email is doing.

Choose it when: you have one channel that clearly matters most, and enough budget for it to be worth their while.

Option 3: Freelancers, one per channel

A writer here, an ads person there, a designer when you need one.

Good at: flexibility and cost. You pay for exactly the skill you need, and stop when you don’t.

Weak at: the join. Four freelancers means four people who don’t talk to each other — and you become the project manager. That’s a real job, and it lands on the person who has the least time for it.

Choose it when: you have well-defined pieces of work and you genuinely enjoy coordinating people.

Option 4: Do it yourself with software

Email platform, ad manager, an SEO tool, a design tool.

Good at: control and learning. Nobody understands your customer better than you, and doing it yourself for a while teaches you what good looks like.

Weak at: it isn’t actually cheaper. The software doesn’t do the work — you do, at night, after everything else. And the parts you don’t enjoy quietly stop happening.

Choose it when: you’re early, the volume is low, and your own time is worth less than the cash.

Option 5: AI marketing agents

This is the newest option, and the one most likely to be oversold, so here’s the plain version.

An AI marketing agent is not a chatbot you type into. It’s a set of scheduled runs — the same marketing job, done on a timetable, whether or not anyone remembers to ask.

The best place to run it is usually the AI software you already pay for. Both Claude and ChatGPT can now run saved tasks on a schedule, so the agent works inside the tool you already open, in a normal conversation you can read, question and steer. Nothing new to buy, nothing new to learn, and if you want to jump in halfway through and change the plan, you just type. If your team would rather get the updates in a chat tool, that works too — but the schedule is the part that matters, not the messaging app.

So a week looks like this: the numbers get checked each morning before anything else happens, the agent does its daily round on search, content, ads and sales, and reports back in plain English. A person still approves anything that goes out to the public.

Good at: job two and job three — the daily doing and the checking. It doesn’t get bored, it doesn’t skip Fridays, and every week of data can be used to make the next week’s decisions better.

Weak at: taste and originality. AI is not the right tool for deciding what your brand stands for, or for making the creative work that makes people feel something. People disagree strongly about this one — plenty of businesses run creative through AI end to end. We’d rather tell you where the line is than pretend it isn’t there.

Choose it when: you know roughly what should happen but nobody has time to make it happen consistently.

The trap in every option: the numbers

Whichever option you pick, the same thing sinks it: bad numbers.

If your revenue figures live in four places and disagree, then the plan is guesswork, the report is fiction, and nobody can tell a good week from a lucky one. This is the boring, unglamorous part that decides whether any of the five options works — and it’s the part almost nobody sells you.

So ask any candidate, agency, freelancer or software vendor the same question: where do your numbers come from, and what happens when you don’t have one? The right answer is “from a single checked source” and “we say it’s unmeasured.” An answer that’s a confident number with no source behind it is the thing to be afraid of — from a person or a machine.

How to choose, in about ten minutes

  • Missing a decision-maker? Fractional CMO.
  • Missing execution on one big channel? Agency.
  • Missing a specific skill, and you have time to manage it? Freelancers.
  • Missing budget more than time? DIY software, for now.
  • Missing consistency — you know what to do, it just doesn’t get done? A system that runs the routine every day.
  • Missing all of the above, and you’re big enough to keep one person busy? Then hire. A good full-time marketer with a real budget beats every option on this page. The alternatives exist because most small businesses can’t keep one properly busy or properly supported — not because hiring is wrong.
